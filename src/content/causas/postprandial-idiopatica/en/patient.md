<aside class="medical-type">
<strong>Type of hypoglycemia produced most often: postprandial.</strong>
This classification is controversial. Symptoms after eating can result from several conditions that are not hypoglycemia.
</aside>

## What it is

Idiopathic postprandial hypoglycemia describes symptoms after eating without a demonstrated cause. In many cases, low glucose is not confirmed.

Whipple's triad requires compatible symptoms, low glucose and improvement after glucose rises. Missing any one element requires another explanation.

## Quick summary

- The term describes a situation without a clear cause.
- Low glucose must coincide with symptoms.
- Several conditions can mimic this experience.
- Home testing may help only when directed.
- Clinical evaluation is needed before rigid treatment.
- Evidence for this category is limited.

## Symptoms

People may report fatigue, hunger, trembling or trouble concentrating. Some describe dizziness or sweating after eating.

The same symptoms can occur with other problems. This is why glucose should be measured during symptoms.

## Why it happens

There is no single confirmed mechanism. Insulin, digestion speed and hormone responses can vary between people.

A rapid glucose decline can cause symptoms without true hypoglycemia. This does not prove that a dangerous disease is present.

## Who may have it

It may occur in people without diabetes who feel ill after meals. It can also occur after metabolic changes or gastric surgery.

Evaluation should exclude early diabetes, tumors, medicines and hormone causes. Age and medical history guide testing.

## How it is diagnosed

A clinician applies Whipple's triad. It seeks symptoms, measures low glucose and observes improvement after glucose rises.

Testing may include a mixed-meal test or supervised monitoring. Home readings do not replace clinical evaluation.

The clinician reviews medicines, alcohol, fasting and surgery history. Insulin and C-peptide tests are useful only in selected settings.

## Treatment

Treatment begins by confirming or excluding hypoglycemia. Restrictive diets should not begin based only on isolated symptoms.

If glucose is not low, the clinician can address alternative explanations. Confirmed hypoglycemia should be managed according to its cause.

## Food and eating

Regular meals can support a stable routine. A reasonable plan may include protein, fiber and measured carbohydrate portions.

Avoid long fasts unless a professional says this is unsafe. Do not start supplement or elimination diets without assessment.

Simple changes can be tracked with symptoms and glucose measurements. If symptoms are severe, stop changes and seek advice.

## What to do during an episode

If conscious, measure glucose when equipment is available. If low, use an adapted 15-15 rule and recheck.

Use glucagon only if the care plan includes it and a caregiver knows how. Recheck glucose after recovery and arrange continued supervision.

Call emergency services if the person is unconscious or cannot swallow. Do not give oral food or drinks to an unconscious person.

## When to seek emergency care

Seek help for persistent confusion, seizures or loss of consciousness. Seek care also if injuries prevent safe recovery at home.

Do not ignore repeated unexplained episodes. Urgent evaluation may exclude serious causes.

## Daily life

Keep a record of timing, meals and symptoms. Record glucose only when a measurement matches the symptoms.

Avoid driving during symptoms or until fully recovered. Ask before intense exercise or hazardous work.

Limit alcohol if it triggers episodes. Pregnancy or breastfeeding requires specialized clinical guidance.

## Questions for your doctor

- Did the measurement confirm low glucose?
- Was Whipple's triad complete?
- What alternative conditions could explain my symptoms?
- Which test is useful in my situation?
- Which changes are safe without unnecessary restriction?
