<aside class="medical-type"><strong>Type of hypoglycemia produced most often: postprandial.</strong> It may occur one to four hours after eating. Fasting episodes can also happen in some people.</aside>

## What it is
Post-bariatric hypoglycemia occurs after weight-loss surgery. Food reaches the intestine faster and hormone signals may increase insulin.

It differs from early discomfort or vomiting after eating. It should be confirmed with low glucose during symptoms.

## Quick summary
- It is more likely after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass.
- It often occurs one to four hours after eating.
- It may resemble late dumping.
- Glucose should be measured during symptoms.
- The plan should fit the previous operation.
- Medicines require specialist review.

## Symptoms
Symptoms include trembling, sweating, hunger and palpitations. Confusion, weakness or sleepiness may also occur.

It can resemble dizziness after a rapid meal. A measurement helps distinguish it.

## Why it happens
Stomach capacity and gastric emptying change after surgery. Intestinal hormones may trigger too much insulin.

A rapid glucose fall may be combined with this response. The exact mechanism needs bariatric and endocrine assessment.

## Who may have it
It is better known after gastric bypass. It may follow sleeve gastrectomy or other operations.

The risk cannot be predicted by time after surgery alone. Many people do not develop hypoglycemia.

## How it is diagnosed
A clinician confirms Whipple's triad. Low glucose must occur with symptoms and improve when glucose rises.

A supervised mixed-meal test may be used. Insulin, C-peptide and drug exposure should be reviewed.

Dumping is distinguished first. Pancreatic tumor suspicion requires specialized evaluation.

## Treatment
The first step is usually food and timing changes. The previous operation determines safe options.

Medicines are considered only if confirmed episodes persist. Pancreatic surgery is exceptional and highly specialized.

## Food and eating
Divide carbohydrate portions into small amounts. Combine food with protein and fiber.

Avoid very sweet meals and sugary drinks. Do not fast for long periods without guidance.

## What to do during an episode
If conscious and able to swallow, take rapid glucose. Wait 15 minutes and recheck.

Repeat once if glucose remains low. Use glucagon only if prescribed and a caregiver knows how.

Do not give food to an unconscious person. Call emergency services.

## When to seek emergency care
Seek help for seizures, loss of consciousness or persistent confusion. Seek care for injury or inability to eat.

## Daily life
Avoid driving with symptoms or low glucose. Plan work and sport with the medical team.

Alcohol may worsen episodes. In pregnancy, seek advice before changing food or medicines.

## Questions for your doctor
- Do symptoms coincide with low glucose?
- Is this dumping or hypoglycemia?
- Which tests do I need?
- Which food changes are safe for my surgery?
- Should I see endocrine or bariatric specialists?
