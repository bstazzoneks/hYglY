## What it is

NIPHS means non-insulinoma pancreatogenous hypoglycemia syndrome. It is also called adult nesidioblastosis.

It is a rare cause of hypoglycemia from excess insulin. Pancreatic beta cells produce insulin without a clearly identified tumor.

## Quick summary

- It is uncommon and may resemble other causes.
- It may follow bariatric or abdominal surgery.
- Confirmation usually requires a critical sample during hypoglycemia.
- Episodes can occur after eating or during fasting.
- Diagnosis should not rely only on symptoms.
- Treatment depends on the confirmed cause.

## Symptoms

Hypoglycemia may cause trembling, sweating and palpitations. It may also cause hunger, confusion, weakness or unusual behavior.

A person can lose consciousness when glucose falls substantially. Symptoms should match a low glucose measurement.

## Why it happens

The body releases too much insulin relative to available glucose. This response may occur after carbohydrate-rich food.

Beta-cell hyperplasia, or an increase in beta cells, may be present. The exact mechanism is not always clear.

## Who may have it

It is described most often after bariatric or abdominal surgery. Gastric changes can alter hormone responses.

Cases without previous surgery also occur. This makes insulinoma and other causes important to exclude.

## How it is diagnosed

A clinician should document low glucose during symptoms. Insulin and C-peptide are usually measured in a critical sample.

Testing may include supervised fasting and post-meal studies. These tests should occur in a specialized medical setting.

Imaging helps exclude a pancreatic tumor. Special studies may locate a region producing excess insulin.

## Treatment

Management depends on severity and the confirmed mechanism. It may include dietary changes and medicines prescribed by a specialist.

Surgery may be considered when symptoms are severe or persistent. No procedure should be chosen without specialized assessment.

## Food and eating

Small, frequent meals may help some people. Combining carbohydrate with protein and fiber may reduce rapid glucose swings.

Avoid long periods without food only when unsafe according to the care team. Sugary drinks should not become a daily prevention strategy.

Vegetables, legumes or whole grains may fit an individualized plan. A professional can adapt the plan to each person.

## What to do during an episode

If the person is conscious and able to swallow, use an adapted 15-15 rule. Take rapid glucose, wait 15 minutes and recheck.

Repeat once if glucose remains low and follow the care plan. Use glucagon if emergency instructions are in place and a caregiver knows how.

Do not give food or drink to an unconscious person. Place them on their side and call for medical help.

## When to seek emergency care

Call emergency services for loss of consciousness or seizures. Seek help for confusion that does not improve promptly.

Seek urgent care for repeated episodes or inability to eat. Do not wait if there is injury or slow recovery.

## Daily life

Plan meals and carry rapid glucose sources. Tell family members and coworkers about warning symptoms.

Avoid driving with symptoms or low glucose. Ask before intense sport or work that involves significant risk.

Alcohol can make glucose recovery harder. Pregnancy requires specialized obstetric and endocrine care.

## Questions for your doctor

- What test confirmed my hypoglycemia?
- Could surgery explain the response?
- Do I need imaging to exclude a pancreatic tumor?
- What plan should I follow during an episode?
- Which medicines or surgery might be appropriate?
