## Clinical summary

NIPHS describes endogenous hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia without insulinoma.

It may relate to bariatric surgery, gastric surgery or nonspecific pancreatic changes.

Diagnosis requires biochemical documentation, tumor exclusion and exclusion of exogenous exposure.

## Definition

The syndrome includes hypoglycemia from excess endogenous insulin. Nesidioblastosis describes beta-cell hyperplasia or abnormal beta-cell distribution.

Do not diagnose it from symptoms alone. The Endocrine Society guideline requires documented low glucose and simultaneous laboratory measurements.

## Epidemiology

It is rare, and prevalence is not established across populations. Associations with bariatric surgery vary by procedure and case selection.

Evidence remains limited by inconsistent registries and variable histologic criteria.

## Pathophysiology

Endogenous insulin may rise after nutrition or through altered pancreatic regulation. Loss of incretin inactivation may contribute after gastric bypass.

Beta-cell hyperplasia does not always explain the phenotype. Physiologic post-surgical changes and dumping should be considered.

## Clinical presentation

| Pattern | Considerations |
|---|---|
| Late postprandial | May follow carbohydrate intake or bariatric surgery |
| Intercurrent | May mimic early or late dumping |
| Fasting | May suggest insulinoma or another endogenous cause |
| Neuroglycopenic | Confusion, abnormal behavior or loss of consciousness |

## Diagnostic criteria

Apply Whipple's triad during a spontaneous or supervised episode.

Confirm low glucose with a validated laboratory method. Interpret insulin, C-peptide and proinsulin in the same critical sample.

Exclude sulfonylureas and exogenous insulin exposure. Endogenous hyperinsulinemia alone does not prove NIPHS.

## Tests and cutoff values

No cutoff values are listed because no universally validated NIPHS-specific thresholds are cited in this record.

| Test | Clinical use | Cautious interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | Documents the episode | Use the sample obtained with symptoms |
| Insulin and C-peptide | Suggest endogenous hypersecretion | Interpret with drug screening |
| Proinsulin | Supports pancreatic hypersecretion | Requires specialist interpretation |
| Beta-hydroxybutyrate | Assesses ketone suppression | Interpret with glucose |
| Supervised fast | Searches for endogenous hypoglycemia | Use a hospital protocol |
| Post-meal or mixed-meal test | Studies nutritional response | May overlap with dumping |
| Abdominal imaging | Excludes tumor or focal lesions | Negative imaging does not exclude small lesions |

## Differential diagnosis

Insulinoma, factitious hypoglycemia, sulfonylureas and meglitinides. Also late dumping, gastroparesia, early diabetes and hypothalamic causes.

Glucagon response may suggest endogenous insulin. Interpretation requires specialized protocols.

## Management

Diet may limit rapid carbohydrate loads and distribute meals. Evidence varies and should be individualized.

Medicines are selected according to physiology and prior surgery. Options may include alpha-glucosidase approaches or secretion modifiers. No doses are listed here.

Surgery may consider partial or focal resection if a hyperfunctional distribution is established. Preoperative and localization studies are essential.

## Complications and prognosis

Neuroglycopenic episodes can cause injury or loss of consciousness. Quality of life may decline because of fear of eating or driving.

Prognosis depends on confirmation, dietary response and severity. Symptoms may persist after initial interventions.

## Follow-up

Document symptoms, glucose measurements and severe episodes. Adjust treatment only with regular endocrine review.

After bariatric surgery, reassess access to and safety of hypoglycemia prevention. Refer for unexplained recurrence.

## Special populations

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and pediatric patients require specialized assessment. Do not apply adult protocols without adaptation.

In older adults, assess polypharmacy, renal function and fall risk. In children, genetic causes may be more relevant.
