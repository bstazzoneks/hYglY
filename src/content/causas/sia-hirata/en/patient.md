<aside class="medical-type"><strong>Type of hypoglycemia produced most often: both.</strong> It may occur after meals and spontaneously. The mechanism requires measured insulin antibodies.</aside>
## What it is
Antibodies bind to insulin made by the body. Insulin may remain bound and later be released.
## Quick summary
- It may follow meals.
- Glucose may alternate high and low.
- It may associate with autoimmune disease or medicines.
- Insulin antibody testing confirms it.
- HLA association requires specific study.
- Treatment depends on severity.
## Symptoms
Trembling, sweating, hunger, confusion and weakness.
## Why it happens
Antibodies release insulin out of sync with glucose.
## Who may have it
Associations include Graves disease, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, monoclonal gammopathies or myeloma. Methimazole, alpha-lipoic acid, captopril and other sulfhydryl drugs have been reported as triggers. HLA-DRB1*04:06 has been described in specific populations, but regional evidence is needed.
## How it is diagnosed
Measure low glucose during symptoms. Check very high insulin, C-peptide and insulin antibodies. Review medicines and autoimmune disease.
## Treatment
A clinician may stop a confirmed trigger. Severe cases may need corticosteroids or immunosuppression.
## Food and eating
Regular small meals may reduce spikes. Do not start restrictive diets alone.
## What to do during an episode
If conscious, take rapid glucose and wait 15 minutes. Repeat once if still low.
## When to seek emergency care
Seek help for loss of consciousness, seizures or persistent confusion.
## Daily life
Avoid driving with symptoms. Discuss exercise, alcohol and pregnancy with the care team.
## Questions for your doctor
- Do antibodies explain episodes?
- Could a medicine trigger them?
- Do I have another autoimmune disease?
- Do I need immunotherapy?
## More information
Reumatology, immunology or endocrine teams according to association.
